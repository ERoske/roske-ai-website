Back in the interRel days, I declared that I was opening all of our webcasts to all of my competitors, and plenty of people told me (mostly politely) that I was giving the whole company away. We were teaching Essbase and EPM for free (considering my own mother once asked whether she really had to spend $49.95 on one of the Look Smarter Than You Are books Tracy McMullen and I wrote just to prove she loved me, the webcasts were the best deal anybody in my family ever got out of me), and anybody at a rival consulting firm could sign up, sit in the back, and take notes on exactly how we did it. At the peak we were running 2 webcasts a week, and we kept that up for multiple years, until we'd become almost an education company that happened to do consulting.

My logic went like this: if I expanded the pie, there'd be more pie for all of us. And if my competitors went out and did a horrible project, the customer wasn't going to say that consulting firm was bad. They were going to say the product sucked, and that would shrink the pie for everybody, including us. (Edward, you built an entire competitive strategy on a dessert, and somewhere a strategy professor just felt a great disturbance in the Force, as if millions of MBA slides cried out in terror and were suddenly silenced… which is 2 franchises in 1 aside, and we haven't even gotten to the one this week is actually about.)

I've been thinking about that pie all week, because [The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping](https://www.thewrap.com/creative-content/movies/the-hunger-games-sunrise-on-the-reaping-review/) opened Friday (it's the young Haymitch one, with Joseph Zada as a teenage Haymitch Abernathy and Ralph Fiennes as President Snow, so Voldemort finally got a whole country to run, and [the critics are split on how that went](https://www.cinemablend.com/movies/sunrise-on-the-reaping-review-roundup-brutal-i-watched-completely-different-movie-joseph-zada)), and [my website](/) put me in the arena this week as a tribute. I read a lot of YA, I must admit, so the machine that picks the theme every Saturday knew exactly where to find me. It's the 50th Games, a Quarter Quell, so the Capitol reaps 48 tributes instead of the usual 24 (twice the paperwork), and the whole show runs on 1 idea: the tributes are each other's problem. The Capitol designs the arena, sends the parachutes, sells the broadcast, and sits up on the balcony with drinks, and none of that gets questioned much, because everybody down in the meadow is busy watching each other.

That's more or less the pitch every leader I've talked to this year has heard about AI. It's a race, your competitors are already in it, and whoever sprints hardest at the Cornucopia on day one wins (allegedly). In that version the other tributes are the bank across the street or the law firm that keeps beating you on pitches (pick your industry, it works for all of them), and the scoreboard is who announced their AI strategy first (somebody sells that scoreboard, by the way, and it's worth a minute to go look at who).

The second half of my pie theory matters even more for AI than it did for Essbase. When a competitor's AI project blows up in public (and some of them will, because the arena is full of things that look beautiful and bite), nobody's going to write that the company implemented it badly. They're going to write that AI doesn't work, your board is going to read it over breakfast, and the pie gets smaller for everyone in your industry at the same time.

The webcasts paid off in a way I never planned, too. The consultants at our competitors kept watching, and when they started thinking about quitting, the first company they called was the one that had been teaching them more than the company they worked for. It turned into a recruiting vehicle. (The way I think about it is LeBron: you can watch him play every game and learn every move he's got, but if you actually want to win the game, you hire LeBron, and yes, I'm aware which one of us in that sentence is 6'9".) The skeptics who said I was giving it all away weren't wrong about the giving, but the customers who were going to do it themselves after watching for free were never going to give us money anyway. (And the prospects who watched everything and then hired somebody else got told the first 3 calls were free, and after that I'd start sending an invoice. I never actually sent one, but I did tell them I would.)

Without spoiling a book that's been out since 2025 (or a movie that's been out since Friday), the tributes worth watching in that series are the ones who quit worrying about the other kids and get curious about the arena itself, mostly where it ends and who's paying for all those parachutes (if you've read it, you know exactly who I mean, and if you haven't, I'm not saying another word). I put a map of the AI version on [the home page](/#arena) this week, sector by sector, and the one I'd worry about most is the Clear Spring, which is the fluent, confident answer that reads exactly the same whether it's right or made up. The edge of that map is the part I'd spend an afternoon on before I spent a dollar at the horn (the how-to is on the home page too, and it mostly comes down to asking it 20 questions you already know the answers to). (The Orchard is a close second, which is the free trial that turns into a price list, and I covered the vendors who dress their products up as AI back on [Halloween](/blog/is-the-ai-you-just-bought-wearing-a-costume/), so I'll spare you the sequel.)

None of this means your competitors don't matter, and if one of them figures out something real, good for them, and I'd go learn it (I spent years teaching mine for free, so I'm in no position to get precious about who learns from whom). They're just not the ones designing the arena, and most of them are as lost in there as you are, so honestly I'd be happy if every one of them got a decent map too.

If you were one of those competitors' consultants sitting in the back of an interRel webcast all those years ago, I'd love to hear where you ended up. And if you're standing in somebody's AI arena right now trying to work out where the edge is, I'm happy to come walk it with you (Edward@Roske.AI).
