The machine that rebuilds [my website](/) every Saturday at 5AM went through the cast of Ebenezer: A Christmas Carol this week (it opened Friday, with Johnny Depp as Scrooge and Ian McKellen as Marley, and [the reviews are split](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/movies/movie-news/ebenezer-reviews-johnny-depp-1236754299/), and I'm staying out of it) and decided that I'm the dead business partner. So right now I'm Marley's Ghost: see-through, a little green, standing in somebody's bedroom in a frock coat with a chain wrapped around me, and hanging off the chain are old ledgers, padlocks, floppy disks, and a beige keyboard from sometime in the 1990s. I'd like to be offended, but I ran a consulting firm for 25 years and wrote 15 books about software, so the chain is pretty much accurate. (Also, Sir Ian and I are technically splitting a role this week, so I'm not going to complain too loudly.)

The book itself is free on [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/46) and it's short. Dickens called the chapters "staves," there are 5 of them, and you can get through the whole thing in an evening, so if the last time you read it was for a class, it's worth reading again before you see the movie (Stave One especially, since Scrooge gets handed a lot more than people remember before a single Spirit shows up).

His nephew Fred comes by the office and makes the case for Christmas, out loud, and it's a genuinely good speech (the clerk in the next room applauds, and nearly loses his job over it). Scrooge's answer is, "You're quite a powerful speaker, sir. I wonder you don't go into Parliament." Then 2 gentlemen come in collecting for the poor, and they've brought numbers: "Many thousands are in want of common necessaries; hundreds of thousands are in want of common comforts." Scrooge asks whether there are no prisons. Then he goes home, double-locks the door, and his business partner, who's been dead for 7 years, walks through it anyway to tell him as an eyewitness exactly how this turns out, and Scrooge tells him he's probably "an undigested bit of beef, a blot of mustard, a crumb of cheese, a fragment of an underdone potato." (As heckles go, that's a really good one, and "There's more of gravy than of grave about you" is the best thing anybody has ever said to a ghost.)

He says "Humbug" 6 times in the book, and all 6 are in Stave One: 2 at his nephew, none at the 2 gentlemen (they just get asked about prisons), and 4 in the general direction of his dead business partner, who's the 1 person on Earth (well, formerly on Earth) who'd done his exact job. I know that because the same machine that drew me as a ghost also counted them.

I learned that one the expensive way back in the interRel days, when a company in Southern California (I'm not going to name them) verbally awarded us a project. We'd won on the solution, we'd won on price, and we'd built them a working demo. Then, in the middle of contracting, they pulled it, and the explanation I got was this: "This other consulting firm takes our CFO to Lakers games. And they sit courtside." When they'd gone to the CFO and told him they were awarding it to us, he'd said, "They've never taken me to a Lakers game." The other firm's bid was triple ours.

What I took away from that is that nobody is ever convinced by numbers. If we were convinced by numbers, everyone would floss and nobody would smoke. People make decisions with their gut, and then they use their head to back it up. (For the record, it didn't teach me that I should go take CFOs to Lakers games. It taught me that sometimes you can be the most logical solution and you're still going to lose, because people buy from people.)

Dickens had that figured out in 1843, which is annoying, because it took me a lost deal and a few decades, and none of his Spirits ever bothers arguing with Scrooge. The first one takes him to a Christmas party his old boss Fezziwig threw, and Scrooge ends up defending Fezziwig out loud ("The happiness he gives, is quite as great as if it cost a fortune") and then goes very quiet, because he's just thought of his own clerk. The second one walks him into Bob Cratchit's house, where Bob toasts him as "the Founder of the Feast" and Mrs. Cratchit says what she'd like to give him instead, and then on to Fred's party, where they're playing a guessing game and the answer is "a disagreeable animal, a savage animal," and it's Scrooge. The third one doesn't say a single word for the entire stave. It just points at things, and it's the most persuasive character in the book (as a guy who talks for a living, I've decided not to think about that too hard).

If you've got a Scrooge at your company when it comes to AI (and if you can't think of who it is… I have some news for you), they've probably seen the deck already, and it had the statistics in it, same as the 2 gentlemen did. I've been doing this since 1995, and every big shift since then had very smart people explaining why it was a fad. (What I said on my show about "AI's a fad" is that it'll end up like personal computers, which are all going to go away someday so we can put everything back on 1 big central computer. That one sort of did end up happening, it just took about 50 years longer than anybody thought.) Somewhere down the hall from your Scrooge there's a Bob Cratchit with a chatbot account nobody approved, and 10 minutes of watching him use it on their own numbers will probably do more than I can do in an email. The best line in the whole book, by the way, is Scrooge's question at the headstone: "Are these the shadows of the things that Will be, or are they shadows of things that May be, only?" He doesn't get an answer. (Yes, I host a show called Asking Good Questions, and yes, I'm aware I just gave the award for best question to a fictional moneylender in a nightshirt.)

Now, in fairness to Scrooge, his skepticism in Stave One is pretty reasonable. If a dead man walked through my bedroom door, "bad cheese" is where I'd start too. A lot of what people say about AI is fair (it does make things up, it might be a bubble, the version they tried in 2023 probably wasn't very good, nobody's data is ready, and so on), so I put 5 of the usual humbugs into [a ledger on the home page](/#ledger) this week, graded honestly, and only 1 of the 5 came out as actual humbug. Dickens also cheats a little. Scrooge's entire follow-through gets about a paragraph ("He became as good a friend, as good a master, and as good a man, as the good old city knew"), and a lot of the conversion is plain fear, since the last thing he's shown is his own name on a headstone. In real life the morning after takes a budget and most of a year, and people laugh at you for a while (Dickens does mention that, to his credit, and says Scrooge let them).
